My name in Chinese

Your name, written in Chinese characters

There are two ways to write an English name in Chinese. One sounds it out. The other gives you a name. Here is the difference, and the tool for the second one.

The two ways

音

Transliteration — how it sounds

Michael becomes 麦克 (màikè), Emily becomes 艾米丽 (àimǐlì). The characters are chosen for sound, not meaning. Everyone will understand you — but to a Chinese reader, 麦克 reads like a microphone brand, not a person.

Best for: filling in a form, ordering coffee, a quick introduction.

名

A real name — what it means

Instead of copying the sound, you get a name built from your birth date and the qualities you want to carry: 张天翔 (zhāng tiān xiáng) — heaven, soaring. The characters mean something before they sound like anything.

Best for: a name you will actually use, a signature, a tattoo you will not regret.

Common questions

Is 麦克 wrong, then?

Not wrong — just incomplete. Transliteration is the standard way foreign names appear in Chinese news and business. But those are labels applied from outside. A name you choose for yourself is different: it should mean something about you, which is what the generator builds.

Can I use my Chinese name legally?

It depends on the country and the document. In China, a long-term visa or residence permit usually wants the transliteration that matches your passport. The generated name is a social name — what friends, teachers and colleagues call you. Both can coexist.

Which characters will I learn to write?

The two or three characters of your given name, plus the surname if you picked one. Each has a stroke-order animation and a printable practice sheet on its character page, reached directly from your result.
